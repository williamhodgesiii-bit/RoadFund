# Turn handshake roads into written, paid-up deals

Road Fund is aimed at a real and growing market that no other product serves, but the market needs less than the current feature list offers. On small private roads the most common and most painful problem is **a neighbor who won't pay**. The cause underneath it is almost always **no written agreement**. That means the product's center should be a clear request for money, a signed agreement and a record everyone can see, not a broad set of HOA-style features. Nobody counts these roads. The Bureau of Transportation Statistics says there is "no consistent data" on private road mileage. County snapshots and some assumption-based math put the number somewhere between the low hundreds of thousands and about a million shared roads. Nearly all of them run on a handshake, one volunteer, paper checks and Venmo. The real competition is free (a group text, Venmo, Zelle). Next come HOA tools at about $144–$650 a year that are priced in tiers starting at 25 units. Money and urgency show up at three moments: a large repair bill, snow season, and a home sale. The mortgage rule of "no agreement, no loan" now applies to far fewer sales than the marketing site implies. It mostly affects Fannie Mae loans in states without one of roughly nine default road-cost statutes, plus individual lenders' extra rules. Older rural neighbors text every day (94% of adults over 50 do), but they are also the main targets of payment-text scams, and their reported text-scam losses rose 61% in one year. Trust therefore has to come from the road captain, from paper, from a pay-by-check option and from calm wording. The audience should stay the same. The product and pricing should change: go from three plans to two, take the late-payer notices out of the $249 plan, stop promoting AI, contractor bids, the pothole map and the tax pack, raise the Road Status Letter to $150, sell the agreement as the fix for neighbors who don't pay, and correct three claims on the marketing site that the research does not support.

## Nobody counts private roads, but rural counties hold hundreds of miles each

No national count exists of private residential roads, private road miles, or homes served by them. The Bureau of Transportation Statistics says plainly that **"no consistent data on private road mileage is available"** ([BTS](https://www.bts.gov/content/public-road-and-street-mileage-united-states-type-surfacea)). FHWA's highway statistics leave out roads that are "privately owned or not publicly maintained" ([FHWA](https://www.fhwa.dot.gov/ohim/hs01/reinfo.htm)). What exists is a set of local snapshots, and they show that private roads are a large share of rural mileage. Maine DEP estimated **about 12,000 miles of private camp roads in lake watersheds alone** ([Maine DEP](https://www.maine.gov/dep/land/watershed/camp/road/index.html)). That is about 53% of the state's 22,848 public road miles ([BTS/ROSAP](https://rosap.ntl.bts.gov/view/dot/85236/dot_85236_DS1.pdf)). A Jefferson County, Missouri council member reported **more than 1,000 miles of private roads**, against only 28 miles the county has accepted for maintenance ([Independent American Communities](https://independentamericancommunities.com/2019/02/18/how-will-local-goverment-deal-crumbling-private-roads/)). Carroll County, Arkansas reports about 296 private miles against 963 county miles ([Eureka Springs Times-Echo](https://www.eurekaspringstimesecho.net/stories/road-maintenance-process-for-responding-to-grievances-dominate-quorum-court-discussion,14731)). These are three non-random data points from different years. Still, all three put private mileage at **tens of percent of local public mileage**, which means it is not a rounding error.

Any national figure has to be built from proxies. About **23 million housing units used private domestic wells in 2010**, more than 50% above the 1990 count ([Data.gov](https://catalog.data.gov/dataset/private-domestic-well-estimates-2010)). That is a reasonable upper-bound pool of low-density homes. Assume 10–25% of them sit on a shared private road or a long shared driveway, and the result is roughly **2.3–5.8 million homes**. At 6–10 homes per road, that is **230,000–970,000 roads**. The percentage range is an assumption that fits only the county snapshots, so it should be checked before it appears in any pitch. The cheapest check is to add up private-road segments in a state E911 road layer. Vermont's centerline layer "includes all private roads" ([VTrans](https://vtransmaps.vermont.gov/Maps/Publications/VTrans_Road_Centerline_User_Guide_20210531.pdf)). The arithmetic matters for strategy. If 1% of that range adopted the $149 plan, subscriptions alone would bring in roughly **$0.3–1.4 million a year**. A cheap subscription can't carry the business by itself, so per-event fees (letters and agreements) have to do real work. The market is growing. By 2000, low-density exurban development covered about **15 times the land area of urban development** ([USGS](https://www.usgs.gov/publications/rural-land-use-trends-conterminous-united-states-1950-2000)). Eleven of the 15 fastest-growing US cities in 2024–25 were exurbs ([Axios](https://www.axios.com/2026/05/19/exurbs-urban-cities-growth-census)). Counties also keep declining to take over new roads.

These roads are organized in three tiers, and only one of them is Road Fund's core market. Maine DEP describes the range: informal agreements, statutory road associations, and nonprofit-corporation associations ([Maine DEP](https://www.maine.gov/dep/land/watershed/roadassociation.html)). The large majority are **handshake roads**, where one person collects money when it's needed. A smaller tier has a **recorded road maintenance agreement (RMA)**, usually created because a lender or title company required one at a sale or refinance. The third tier uses public assessment tools: California permanent road divisions ([Marin County](https://publicworks.marincounty.org/permanent-road-divisions-program)), Michigan township special assessment districts started by a 51% petition ([MCL Act 139](https://renderpdf.legislature.mi.gov/documents/mcl/pdf/MCL-ACT-139-OF-1972.pdf)), Oregon special road districts ([Deschutes County](https://deschutescounty.gov/road/page/special-road-districts)) and Washington road improvement districts ([RCW 36.88](https://app.leg.wa.gov/rcw/default.aspx?cite=36.88)). These put the charge on the property-tax bill, so the county already collects the money. That makes them poor prospects. They also require petitions, hearings and engineering reports, which is too much for a 3–30 home road. **Road Fund's opening is the space between a handshake and a special district.**

### Nine states set a default share rule but leave collection to the neighbors

At least nine states have a statute that sets a default duty to share costs, or a court process to set one, when neighbors have no written agreement. Michigan adds an assessment route. Most of these laws passed between 2010 and 2021. Realtors and title associations pushed for them so that private-road homes could qualify for Fannie Mae loans without a separate agreement. Fannie Mae waives the agreement where "the property is located within a state that has statutory provisions that define the responsibilities" of owners ([Fannie Mae B4-1.3-04](https://selling-guide.fanniemae.com/sel/b4-1.3-04/site-section-appraisal-report)).

| State | Statute | Default split | What the app should preset |
|---|---|---|---|
| California | Civ. Code §845 ([Justia](https://law.justia.com/codes/california/code-civ/division-2/part-2/title-3/chapter-2/section-845)) | In proportion to use; suit after written demand; no forced paving | Split by distance or use; written demand letter |
| Oregon | ORS 105.170–.185 ([LegalClarity](https://legalclarity.org/ors-105-175-easement-maintenance-and-cost-sharing-rules/)) | By use, including how often and vehicle weight | Split by use |
| Connecticut | C.G.S. §47-42f ([Justia](https://law.justia.com/codes/connecticut/title-47/chapter-822/section-47-42f)) | In proportion to benefit received | Split by benefit; correct the citation in `lib/states.ts` |
| Vermont | 19 V.S.A. §2702 ([VT Legislature](https://legislature.vermont.gov/statutes/fullchapter/19/027)) | "Contribute rateably" | Equal or by use |
| New Hampshire | RSA 231:81-a ([Justia](https://law.justia.com/codes/new-hampshire/2022/title-xx/title-231/section-231-81-a/)) | "Contribute equitably" | Equal or by use (missing from the app) |

| Missouri | RSMo 228.341–.369 ([Justia](https://law.justia.com/codes/missouri/title-xiv/chapter-228/section-228-341)) | A court sets a plan: equal, by value, frontage or use | Show the court route (missing from the app) |
| Michigan | 1972 PA 139 ([MCL](https://renderpdf.legislature.mi.gov/documents/mcl/pdf/MCL-ACT-139-OF-1972.pdf)) | No duty between neighbors; township assessment after a 51% petition | Explain the petition route only |

Searches found no default statute in Washington, which refiled its bill repeatedly ([WA HB 2165](https://lawfilesext.leg.wa.gov/Biennium/2019-20/Htm/Bills/House%20Bills/2165.htm)). None turned up in Virginia ([Va. Code §55.1-307](https://law.lis.virginia.gov/vacode/55.1-307)), West Virginia, which only asked for a study ([WV SCR 47](https://www.wvlegislature.gov/Bill_Text_HTML/2020_sessions/RS/bills/SCR47%20INTR.htm)), Idaho ([Stoel Rives](https://www.stoel.com/insights/publications/in-idaho-an-easement-owner-has-the-duty-to-mainta)), Montana, Texas ([Texas A&M AgriLife](https://agrilife.org/texasaglaw/2015/11/30/questions-from-tiffanys-desk-who-is-required-to-maintain-an-easement/)), North Carolina, New York or Massachusetts. Some courts apply a "modern" common-law rule of equitable contribution, as an Arizona appeals court did in *Freeman v. Sorchych* ([Osborn Maledon](https://www.omlaw.com/azapp-blog/postings/2011/multiple-dominant-estate-holders-sharing-use-of-a-common-easement-must-equitably-contribute-to-the/)). About 35 states were not confirmed either way. Two strategic points follow. First, these statutes say neighbors owe a share, but apart from Maine's statutory associations, which can record a lien, they give no way to collect it except a lawsuit, often after a **written demand** (California, Maine, Connecticut). That gap between "the law says you owe" and "here is an easy way to bill and collect" is exactly where Road Fund fits. Second, the app's state table currently lists only six states. It should add New Hampshire, Wisconsin and Missouri, and preset the default split each state's law uses.

## A neighbor who won't pay is the top pain, and a missing agreement is its cause

No survey of private-road owners exists. The ranking below counts independent sources in forums, lawyer Q&A sites, agency guides and court records, and rates intensity from what people describe. It is not a random sample, but the pattern holds across every source type.

| Rank | Pain point | Sources found | How intense | What it asks of the product |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Neighbor won't pay | ~14 | Others cover the share; debts grow for years; trials and appeals | A clear request, reminders, written notices, a record a judge would accept |
| 2 | No written or recorded agreement (the root cause) | ~15 | "Can't force them to pay"; blocks some loans | Agreement drafting and signatures on every plan |
| 3 | Snow plowing | ~10 | Overnight deadline, every winter, public fights | Season billing, collect before plowing, pay the contractor once |
| 4 | Fights over a fair share | ~9 | Leads straight to nonpayment; paving fights | One formula agreed in advance and shown on every bill |
| 5 | Grading, gravel, drainage | ~9 | Yearly cost plus large one-time bills | Project vote, money pledged before work, a savings target |
| 6 | Lender wants a recorded agreement at sale | ~12, mostly agents and lenders | Closing stalls or the loan is denied | Road Status Letter and agreement package |
| 7 | One neighbor acts alone, then bills the rest | ~7 | "Pay for what I never agreed to?" | Consent before work, invoice attached to the bill |
| 8 | One volunteer carries the load | ~6 | The volunteer pays for everyone else | Pay contractors from road money; hand off the captain role |
| 9 | Legal escalation | ~7 | $3,500+ in costs; appeals | A trail of written demands; small-claims packet |
| 10–13 | New owners unaware; poor records; heavy users; liability | ~4–5 each | Surprises at purchase; bills with no invoice | Mostly covered by rows 1–7 |
| 14 | Road money in someone's personal Venmo | 0 direct | Only advisors raise it | Reassure; don't headline |

The top two rows are one problem seen from two sides. Lawyers answering residents nearly always start by asking what the recorded documents say. The usual honest answer is: "If not you can't force them to pay" ([Avvo](https://www.avvo.com/legal-answers/can-neighbors-on-a-private-road-be-forced-to-contr-3397115.html)). Maine DEP's guide says informal roads have "no legal mechanism for collecting maintenance costs" from owners who won't pay voluntarily ([Maine DEP guide](https://www.maine.gov/dep/land/watershed/road_assoc_guide_2020_edit.pdf)). The stories are specific. One Maine member hadn't paid since 2016 despite 10% annual interest in the bylaws ([MARA](https://www.maineroads.org/page-239253/13316272)). In a retold 2026 Lake Michigan case, a neighbor covered a widow's **$550 seasonal plow fee** ([TwistedSifter](https://twistedsifter.com/2026/04/elderly-neighbor-in-unofficial-hoa-refuses-to-pay-her-portion-of-the-snow-plow-fees-so-another-neighbor-pays-it-on-her-behalf/)). A Minnesota dispute went through a court trial and then an appeal ([Minn. Ct. App. A22-0764](https://cases.justia.com/minnesota/court-of-appeals/2023-a22-0764.pdf)). One summary says it started from an unpaid quarter of a $3,200 repair, but a second search could not confirm that figure. The sums are small and the fights are long. That points to a problem of **coordination and documentation, not ability to pay**. It also supports the site's FAQ line that most neighbors pay once there is a clear amount and a reason. A shared-road guide notes that many people who don't pay "simply haven't been asked clearly and specifically" ([Gravel Road Guide](https://gravelroadguide.pages.dev/guides/shared-road-agreement)).

Snow is the most emotional trigger because it runs on a deadline. An Adirondack lawyer counts plowing as "the source of conflict on at least three private roads" around Loon Lake ([adkwhaler](https://substack.com/@adkwhaler/note/c-92406352?r=emoic)). Another neighbor announced a lawsuit in the "village group chat" ([Cheezburger](https://cheezburger.com/44872709/look-at-the-undriveable-road-hes-suing-me-over-neighbor-roasted-in-the-village-group-chat-when-he)). Towns generally cannot step in. New York's Comptroller says a town "may not plow and sand this private road on a regular basis" ([NY OSC](https://www.osc.ny.gov/legal-opinions/opinion-90-59)). Fair-share fights cluster around changes such as paving, heavy users, or the house nearest the entrance. Many roads deliberately pick a simple equal split because it causes fewer arguments ("If there isn't a rule telling you what to do anywhere, you divide evenly") ([Bogleheads](https://www.bogleheads.org/forum/viewtopic.php?t=469753)). A formula everyone agreed to in advance matters more than a precise one. Decisions made by one neighbor also go wrong. In a retold Minnesota case, a neighbor paved half the road over an objection and then asked for money without an invoice ([Cheezburger](https://cheezburger.com/46211077/minnesota-homeowner-declines-neighbors-plan-to-pave-half-their-private-road-neighbor-does-it-anyway)). California §845 does not make neighbors pay for improvements they didn't agree to ([Romo Law Group](https://romolawgroup.com/blog/california-shared-private-road-maintenance-civil-code-845/)). Whether a neighbor contributes often depends on "the maintenance history … and what notice was provided" ([Lebovitz Law](https://lebovitzlaw.com/real-estate-issues/common-driveway-easement-pennsylvania/)).

Today roads get by with one designated contact who deals with the contractor and splits the bill ([H&J Property Care](https://hjpropertycare.com/private-road-snow-plowing)), a group chat, and occasionally a meeting followed by door-to-door collection. In one Bogleheads case, "All 21 homeowners paid their share" ([Bogleheads](https://www.bogleheads.org/forum/viewtopic.php?t=329461)). When residents say what they wish they had, they describe Road Fund's best features almost exactly: an agreement that binds future owners, a reserve so repairs don't require a scramble for cash, **everyone paying before the work starts**, an invoice before anyone pays, and a formal vote ([Bogleheads](https://www.bogleheads.org/forum/viewtopic.php?t=469753); [TractorByNet](https://www.tractorbynet.com/forums/threads/shared-private-road-maintenance-fund.237794/)). Three findings are just as important because they are absent. No resident complaint was found about money sitting in a volunteer's Venmo; that worry comes from advisors ([Banking Crowded](https://bankingcrowded.com/all-blogs/venmo-for-associations/)). No partition or quiet-title disputes turned up. No resident used the word "burnout." The volunteer does carry the load, but residents describe it as covering other people's shares, not as exhaustion. Messaging should use their words.

## Checks, Venmo and a group text are the competition, not software

Real road and small-association pages show the actual setup: a volunteer treasurer, checks made out to the group, and a payment app added alongside the checks rather than replacing them. Franklin Valley Association added Venmo in 2023 and asks members to **add 3% for fees** ([Franklin Valley](http://franklinvalley.org/2023/05/venmo-now-available-for-association-dues-payment/)). Pheasant View accepts Zelle and "is not set up for EFT or ACH" ([Pheasant View](https://www.pheasantview.org/treasurer)). A Westport, Connecticut road association collects **about $400 per home per year**, mostly for plowing, "on an informal basis" ([Westport CT](https://westportct.gov/home/showpublisheddocument/99318)). Searches for road-association software found only public-works pavement tools, single-home snow-plow booking apps, and agreement templates. The one vendor that names "Road Associations or Private Road Maintenance Associations" as customers is MoneyMinder, a bookkeeping tool for volunteer treasurers ([MoneyMinder](https://moneyminder.com/hoa/)). The closest consumer product is Venmo Groups, launched in 2023, which logs shared expenses and works out shares ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2023/11/14/venmo-gets-a-new-way-to-split-expenses-among-groups-like-clubs-teams-trip-buddies-and-more)). It cannot handle neighbors who don't use apps, checks, or a record that carries over when a house sells.

Road Fund's economics hold up but are not the lowest, and the marketing site says otherwise. Take a 10-home road at $400 a home ($4,000 a year). The $149 plan plus $10 of per-payment fees comes to about **4% of the budget**. PayHOA would take 13–16%, HOA Express with payments 4.5–6%, and SpruceHOA about 3.6%. On one $400 bank payment, Stripe's 0.8% rate ([Stripe](https://stripe.com/payments/us-bank-payments)) plus Road Fund's $1 comes to about $4.20, against PayHOA's $1.95 flat. On a card, Road Fund comes to about $12.90 and PayHOA $13.50. The site's claim that "the cheapest HOA software for a 10-home road runs $468 to $588 a year" (`app/(site)/pricing/page.tsx`, and the "$468–$588" figure in the comparison table in `app/(site)/page.tsx`) is wrong, because SpruceHOA and HOA Express cost less. The defensible comparison is PayHOA, the common self-managed choice, at about $530–$650 a year. What actually sets Road Fund apart is the set of jobs nobody else does: a fair split with a bill for each home, one record that covers checks, Venmo and cards together, a letter for closings, a guided path from a handshake to a recorded agreement, and a polite trail of reminders and demands. Those five jobs are the moat. The HOA-style extras are not.

## Older neighbors text every day and are trained to distrust payment texts

Phones are no longer the barrier. AARP's 2026 survey found **90% of adults 50+ own a smartphone** (up from 55% in 2016), and **94% text regularly**. Texting has overtaken email as their main channel ([AARP](https://www.aarp.org/pri/topics/technology/internet-media-devices/2026-technology-trends-older-adults/)). The gaps are at the edges. **78% of adults 65+** own a smartphone ([Pew](https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/fact-sheet/mobile/)), so about one in five does not. Rural home broadband is 71%, against 84% in suburbs ([Pew](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2026/01/08/internet-use-smartphone-ownership-digital-divides-in-u-s/)). About one in five rural adults goes online only by phone ([Pew](https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/fact-sheet/internet-broadband/)). More than 9 million rural Americans still lack wired 100/20 service ([FCC](https://docs.fcc.gov/public/attachments/FCC-26-55A1.pdf)). The pay page has to load fast on a weak cell signal, and the oldest neighbors still need a paper option.

Paper checks are still normal for exactly this kind of payment. The Atlanta Fed reports that more than half of consumers write a check at least once a year and about a third write one monthly. **Check use rises with age**, and checks are most common for paying contractors and local taxes ([Atlanta Fed](https://www.atlantafed.org/research-and-data/publications/policy-hub-macroblog/2026/10/08/surveying-consumers-use-of-personal-checks)). That is close to what road dues are. Adults 55+ still use cash for 19% of their payments ([FRB Services](https://www.frbservices.org/news/press-releases/051325-findings-from-2025-diary-of-consumer-payment-choice/)). Only **2% of adults 60+ have no bank account** ([Federal Reserve](https://www.federalreserve.gov/newsevents/pressreleases/other20260513a.htm)), so almost everyone could pay from a bank account. Trust is the obstacle. In a 2024 survey, 56% named security and trust as their top reason for not paying directly from a bank ([Federal Reserve FEDS Note](https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/notes/feds-notes/pay-by-bank-and-the-merchant-payments-use-case-benefits-20250707.html)). Baby boomers use mobile banking much less than younger people ([Kansas City Fed](https://www.kansascityfed.org/research/payments-system-research-briefings/are-us-consumers-ready-to-use-pay-by-bank-at-the-point-of-sale/)). The pay page currently preselects bank payment, which asks the most hesitant group to sign in to their bank on a page they've never seen.

The sharpest risk is that Road Fund's main channel looks like a scam. The FTC told Congress that adults 60+ reported **$3 billion in fraud losses in 2025**, five times the 2020 figure, and that their **reported losses to text scams rose 61% in a year**, often from texts posing as banks or familiar brands ([AccountsRecovery.net](https://www.accountsrecovery.net/2026/10/22/ftc-older-adults-reported-3-billion-in-fraud-losses-in-2025/); [PYMNTS](https://pymnts.com/cybersecurity/fraud-prevention/2026/ftc-data-shows-investment-scams-fuel-fivefold-surge-in-seniors-fraud-losses)). The FTC teaches people not to tap links in unexpected texts, to treat pressure to act fast as "one of the top tricks in the scammer playbook," and to check with the sender using contact details they already know ([FTC](https://consumer.ftc.gov/consumer-alerts/2025/04/unexpected-text-scam)). It also warns specifically about official-looking payment demands ([FTC](https://consumer.ftc.gov/consumer-alerts/2026/04/text-about-traffic-violation-probably-scam)). A text that says "pay here," with a deadline and a court threat, matches that scam pattern exactly. Road Fund's final-notice template does both: "within 10 days … the road will file in small claims court" (`lib/templates.ts`). Notices like that belong on a dated paper letter. A letter also satisfies the written-demand step in the statutes.

Usability research sets firm rules. Users 65+ are **43% slower on websites** and struggle with small text, small tap targets, dropdowns and basic web jargon such as "homepage" ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/usability-for-senior-citizens/); [NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/define-techy-words-old-users/)). WCAG 2.2 sets 24 px as the minimum tap target and 44 px as the stricter level ([W3C](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/quickref/)). GOV.UK's "one thing per page" pattern suits users with low confidence ([GOV.UK](https://designnotes.blog.gov.uk/2015/07/03/one-thing-per-page/)). Unexpected costs cause about 48% of avoidable checkout abandonment, and being forced to create an account causes about 26% ([Growthegy summary of Baymard](https://www.growthegy.com/2026/05/26/cart-abandonment-science-why-customers-leave-checkout/)). People trust a familiar brand name more than security jargon, and visual glitches on a payment form make people think it has been hacked ([Baymard](https://baymard.com/blog/perceived-security-of-payment-form)). AI is now a trust problem. 57% say AI-generated content has made them less trusting of brand messages (Gartner), and 32% would trust a brand less if its content were AI-made, against 15% who would trust it more (YouGov/Meltwater) ([Ringer Sciences](https://ringersciences.com/articles/ringer-weekly-2026-08-10-ai-content-trust-backlash.html)). About 60% of adults 50+ say technology isn't designed with them in mind ([AARP](https://www.aarp.org/pri/topics/technology/internet-media-devices/2026-technology-trends-older-adults/)).

The neighbor page already gets the basics right. It has no login, shows fees before the pay button, says "Payments handled by Stripe," and offers a fallback to paying by check. The gaps are in the messages and in a few word choices. Text templates sign off with only the captain's first name ("– Jim"). They include "Thanks!" and "Tap to vote." No text tells the neighbor how to check that it's real without using the link. The bank-payment checkbox says "I authorize this one-time bank debit." Most important, nothing makes sure a neighbor hears about Road Fund from the captain before the first link arrives. **Trust comes from the captain, not from Road Fund.** The product's job is to make the captain look credible and easy to check.

## Money moves at three moments: the big repair, the first snow and the sale

Routine costs are modest and uneven. Public data on gravel roads puts upkeep at about **$1,380–$5,452 per mile per year** ([Iowa State InTrans](https://intrans.iastate.edu/app/uploads/2018/03/RukashazaTreatments.pdf)). For a quarter- to half-mile road shared by 5–10 homes, that works out to roughly $35–$550 a home before plowing. With plowing, it is roughly $100–$900 per home per year, which is consistent with Westport's $400. Neighbor-organized plow contracts cost **$475–$550 a season** ([St. Paul](https://www.stpaul.gov/sites/default/files/2023-11/Alley%20Berkeley%20St%20Clair%20Snelling%20Macalester%20Plowing%20Contract.Deb_.pdf)), and small private roads "can exceed $1,000 per season" ([The HOA Guide](https://www.thehoaguide.com/who-is-responsible/hoa-road-maintenance/)). People are price-sensitive here, but they do accept a small fee per house for the person who handles everything. One Bogleheads volunteer added one, and nobody objected ([Bogleheads](https://www.bogleheads.org/forum/viewtopic.php?t=469753)).

Big repairs are where collections break down. Regrading runs **$1,200–$3,800** ([Design Transition Studio](https://designtransitionstudio.com/cost-regrading-gravel-driveway/)). Culverts run **$500–$3,500 each** ([AsphaPro](https://asphapro.com/blog/how-much-does-it-cost-to-pave-a-private-road.html); [HPC North Georgia](https://hpcnorthgeorgia.com/how-much-does-it-cost-to-grade-a-driveway/)). Building a quarter-mile gravel road runs $5,300–$40,000, depending on the source ([Angi](https://www.angi.com/articles/how-much-cost-build-road-property.htm)). One Virginia group faced nearly **$100,000 split among about 12 homes** ([Independent American Communities](https://independentamericancommunities.com/2019/01/05/reader-affordable-solutions-pave-hoa-private-roads/)). A bill several times the yearly budget is when an informal road needs a vote, money collected up front and a written record. Road Fund's free plan already makes the second bill of the year the point where a road upgrades, which is the right place for it. Snow is the other urgent moment. It is late October, so snow-state roads are deciding right now how this winter's plowing gets split.

The home sale is the one moment with a third party paying and a hard deadline, but it applies to fewer sales than it used to.

The evidence that sales stall is real but mostly from before these changes. Maine Realtors testified that the requirement "often resulted in the collapse of the transaction" ([Maine Legislature](https://legislature.maine.gov/testimony/resources/JUD20210218Hiestand132580764843702852.pdf)). Problems surfaced "days before closing" ([Maine Realtor](https://digitaleditions.sheridan.com/article/Capitol+Business/3633899/654962/article.html)), and "a single holdout can derail your entire purchase" ([Living in Davie](https://livingindavieflorida.com/davie-acreage-private-road-maintenance-agreement/)). Agents in Michigan, North Carolina and Georgia still write about it ([Sprick Homes](https://sprickhomes.com/blog/the-private-road-detail-that-can-stall-a-hamilton-mi-home-sale); [Aspyre Realty](https://aspyrerealtygroup.com/private-road-maintenance-agreements-nc/); [Guide to Blue Ridge](https://guidetoblueridge.com/blog/in-fannin-county-the-road-isnt-the-risk-the-paperwork-is)). No source measures how often it happens today. The delay comes from people, not from drafting: tracking down every owner, and holdouts who want their own lawyer. Getting an agreement in place takes "a few weeks to several months" ([Bridge Legal](https://bridgelegal.org/private-road-maintenance-agreement-how-obtain-use/)). Every program still checks for a **recorded permanent easement** and an all-weather road. The Road Status Letter should report both, not only dues.

Willingness to pay has clear reference prices at the sale. States that cap HOA estoppel or resale certificates set the cap at **$250 in Maryland, $275 in Washington, about $299 in Florida and $375 in Texas**, plus update fees of $75–$100 ([Havn](https://havnhq.com/features/resale-certificates/maryland); [CommunityPay WA](https://www.communitypay.us/concepts/washington-resale-certificate-requirements/); [CommunityPay FL](https://www.communitypay.us/concepts/florida-estoppel-certificate-requirements/); [CommunityPay TX](https://www.communitypay.us/resale-certificates/texas/)). Standard contracts usually make that fee a seller cost ([Barnes Walker](https://barneswalker.com/estoppel-letters-florida/)). No equivalent letter exists for informal roads. The closest thing is Maine associations recording a lien for unpaid dues that gets paid off at closing ([MARA](https://www.maineroads.org/page-239253/13136495)). So Road Fund would be creating a new category, not slotting into an existing practice. Agreements anchor at $200–$300 at the low end ([CPO Experts](https://www.cpoexperts.com/post/road-maintenance-agreements-can-hamper-your-home-sale)) and up to $2,500 for complex multi-party drafting. Residents also pay for quick legal answers, as the number of private-road threads on JustAnswer shows ([JustAnswer](https://www.justanswer.com/real-estate-law/8n2xj-neighbor-won-t-pay-fair-share-washoe-county-nevada-private.html)). Litigation over road shares costs far more than the shares themselves, as with one neighbor's $3,500 survey ([Justia](https://answers.justia.com/question/2024/04/23/have-a-prescriptive-easement-but-my-neig-1011527)). Spending is low and steady for ordinary upkeep, and it jumps whenever a deadline, a third-party payer or a legal threat appears.

### Keep the audience, change the position

The strongest alternative to the current plan is to sell to real-estate agents, closing attorneys and title companies. That is where willingness to pay is highest, a third party pays, there is a deadline, and agents already publish content about private roads. It still falls short as the main business. It happens only at sales. Statutes and agency changes since 2019 have made it less common. No letter practice exists yet, so the category would have to be built from scratch. Most important, a status letter is only worth what the road's records are worth, and those records come from a captain using Road Fund all year. The captain stays the customer, and closing professionals become a **sales channel**. The position should be: **"Road Fund keeps your road's money and paperwork in one place, so the neighbor who organizes things stops paying for everyone else."** The hero line "Stop fronting the money for your road" already says this. The supporting messages are, in order: ask once, clearly; put the deal in writing, because a signed agreement is what makes a share collectible; everyone sees the same numbers; and you're ready when a house sells. In the nine statute states, the site can say truthfully that "your state's law already says neighbors share road costs" and name the formula. Elsewhere, the honest message is that "without a written agreement, you probably can't make a neighbor pay."

### Copy rules and words to retire

Every money message opens with the amount, what it's for and the date ("Your share is $240 for the gravel delivered June 3, due July 1"). Sentences stay short (about 20 words at most) and speak to "you," as the Federal Plain Language Guidelines advise ([Federal Plain Language Guidelines](https://global.oup.com/us/companion.websites/fdscontent/uscompanion/us/static/companion.websites/9780199379996/pdf/ch2/FederalPLGuidelines.pdf)). Money messages never contain exclamation marks, urgency, "account suspended" language, link shorteners or QR codes. They always use the same readable web address and name a real person and the road. Fees appear as exact dollars before the pay button. Checks never cost extra; in a European survey, 65% said paper shouldn't cost more ([Two Sides](https://lovepaper.org/2025-trend-tracker-survey-consumers-want-the-choice/)). Error messages say how to fix the problem and offer a person to call ([GOV.UK](https://design-system.service.gov.uk/components/error-message/)). Marketing drops superlatives, invented statistics and AI headlines. It uses real road names (with permission), real numbers, and testimonials only with a name, a town and a road.

### Go-to-market: plowing season, closings, plow contractors and Maine

Start with snow, because the season is starting. A "split this winter's plowing" campaign for snow-belt roads in Maine, Vermont, New Hampshire, northern Michigan and Wisconsin, the Adirondacks, Minnesota and the Mountain West matches the most urgent recurring pain. It should offer collection before the plow arrives and one payment to the contractor. Recruit plow and grading contractors as a channel. They already work through "one designated contact" for groups of neighbors who split costs ([H&J Property Care](https://hjpropertycare.com/private-road-snow-plowing)), and getting paid once by the road instead of chasing several homes is a pitch they understand. Next, give agents, title companies and closing attorneys a free verification page and a "before you list on a private road" kit, starting in states with no statute and many private roads (North Carolina, Georgia, Virginia, Washington, Michigan, Texas). That is where agents are already writing about stalled sales. Make Maine the first state to fully support. It has a statute with majority votes, one vote per parcel and 30-day notice ([23 M.R.S. §3101](https://legislature.maine.gov/statutes/23/title23sec3101.html)), a DEP guide, and an organized members' alliance (MARA) whose forum holds some of the most direct resident evidence found. A Maine statutory-association mode, plus a short owner survey run through MARA, would test the pain ranking above at almost no cost. Search content should answer the questions residents actually type into lawyer Q&A sites: "neighbor won't pay for private road," "how to split private road costs," and "[state] private road law."

## Conclusion

The legal trend changes what Road Fund is selling. Each new default statute removes some of the mortgage pressure that made agreements urgent, and each one also writes into law a duty to pay that comes with almost no way to collect short of a lawsuit after written demand. So the lasting value is less "you need this to sell your house" and more "this collects what the law, or your neighbors' signatures, already says you're owed." Every feature can be judged by one question: does it produce a record a neighbor, a buyer's lender or a small-claims judge would accept? Signed agreements, consent before work, dated notices, receipts and the status letter pass. Pothole maps, quote comparison and tax packs do not.

Two strategic risks matter more than any feature. First, the product borrows its credibility from one volunteer. If a text can be mistaken for a scam, or "the road's account" turns out to be Jim's personal account, the neighbor's trust fails first and the captain's reputation goes with it. Second, the business is small per road. The subscription can't carry it alone, so letters and agreements have to grow alongside it. The biggest unknown is how often closings stall today. Before spending heavily on the closing channel, run two cheap checks: a count of private-road segments from Vermont's E911 layer, and a MARA owner survey. Those would turn a 4x-wide estimate of the market into a number the team can plan against.
